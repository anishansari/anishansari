02

The five
parties

One at a time. Nobody gets skipped.

Party 1 of 5

Consumer

The cardholder. Owns the card, sits at a browser, starts the purchase.

Their money is the money that eventually moves.

Party 2 of 5

Merchant

The seller's website. Runs the shopping cart, and runs the merchant software that starts the payment request.

Note what the merchant is not: nobody who can approve anything.

Party 3 of 5

Clearinghouse

The middleman. Takes the merchant's request and routes it into the banking network.

It holds nobody's money. It only carries messages and validates them.

Party 4 of 5

Merchant Bank

also called the acquirer

Holds the merchant's account. This is where the money lands.

Party 5 of 5

Issuing Bank

also called the issuer

Gave the consumer the card. Checks the account and the balance, and bills them later.

This is the only party that can say yes or no.

Never mix these two up

Issuer issues
the card.
Acquirer acquires
the money.

Say it back to me.

All five,
on one map

03

The cycle.
Six steps.

One step per slide. Nothing appears before it happens.

Step 1 of 6

Consumer makes
a purchase

Item goes in the cart. Card details go in the form. Submit.

Step 2 of 6

SSL/TLS makes
the line secure

A secure tunnel through the internet to the merchant server. The number is now unreadable to anyone in between.

Step 3 of 6

Merchant software
contacts the clearinghouse

The merchant now has the card information — and immediately hands the problem to someone else, over a secure line.

Step 4 of 6

Clearinghouse verifies
with the issuing bank

Is this account real? Is the balance enough?

Stop here

Step 4 is the
only place a
decision happens.

Every other step is just carrying. One bank, one yes or no.

And the merchant is told no — never why.

05

When it
breaks

Where the chain snaps tells you who is responsible.

Failure 1

Card declined

Breaks at step 4. Code 05 — do not honour. The issuer refused. The merchant is told only that it failed.

Failure 2

Insufficient funds

Also step 4. Code 51. Same break point, different reason — and the merchant still cannot tell the two apart.

Merchant ko sirf itna
pata chala: "declined".

Bank ne balance nahi bataya. Izzat bach gayi.

That privacy is by design — the shop is told no, never how much you had.

Failure 3

Fraud flag

Step 4 again. The issuer's risk engine did not like the pattern — wrong country, odd hour, unusual amount.

Failure 4

Gateway timeout

Breaks at step 3. Nobody ever decided anything. The request went out and nothing came back.

The one that actually hurts

A timeout is
worse than a
decline.

A decline is an answer.
A timeout means the merchant does not know whether you were charged.

This is why every real payment system needs idempotency keys and a reconciliation job.

Failure 5

TLS handshake
fails

The good failure. Breaks at step 2 — the card number never left the browser. Nothing was charged, nothing was exposed.

Where it broke,
who decided

Step 2
TLS fails
Decided by: the browser.
Data never left.
Step 3
Timeout
Decided by: nobody.
Outcome unknown.
Step 4
Declined · no funds · fraud
Decided by: the issuing bank.
The only judge in the system.

Three break points. Notice steps 5 and 6 never fail — by then it is already agreed.

What the notes
leave out

Exam answer stays as it is. This is the version that is actually running.

Step 5 is really
two events

First
Authorization
At checkout, one second. The issuer reserves the amount and returns an approval code.

You see a pending hold.
Later
Capture / settlement
When the merchant ships, or in a nightly batch. The money actually moves.

You see a posted transaction.
You have all seen this

Book a hotel.
Cancel it.

The hold appears, then quietly vanishes a few days later.
That is an authorization that was never captured.

Buy a book online and it posts the next day — authorization, then capture.

Missing from the diagram

The OTP screen

The cycle verifies the account. It never verifies the person.

3-D Secure adds that challenge between steps 3 and 4. If you pass it, blame for fraud moves from the merchant to the bank.

Merchants adopted it for the liability shift, not the security.

07

Four
limitations

Straight from your notes. Each one is examinable.

Limitation 1

Security

No card, no signature, no impression.

Anyone who copies the digits can reproduce everything the system asks for.

Limitation 2

Merchant risk

In a CNP transaction the merchant carries the chargeback.

A fraudulent order costs them the goods and the money. The bank loses nothing.

Limitation 3

Cost

Roughly 2–3% plus a fixed fee, every single transaction.

Sell something for NPR 10 and the fee eats it.

Micropayments are impossible on this system. That is not a bug — it is arithmetic.

Hisaab lagao

NPR 10 ka samaan.
NPR 4 ki fee.

🧮😵‍💫

Ab batao, business kaise chalega?

This is not a joke about banks. It is the exact arithmetic that forced e-cash and wallets to be invented.

Limitation 4

Social equity

A card needs a bank relationship and a credit history.

No card, no e-commerce. Millions of people are locked out of the entire system by step zero.

Every limitation is
the next topic

Cost
→ stored value & e-cash
Low-value transactions, no bank verification per payment.
Equity
→ wallets & P2P
eSewa, Khalti, Google Pay. No credit history needed.
Security
→ SET protocol
Dual signature, so the merchant never sees the card number at all.

Nothing in this unit is a random list. Each one solves the previous one's problem.
